SphereEdit

Spherical semantic editing in diffusion models

Konate, Hamidi, Dolatabadi, Rudzicz, Seyyed-Kalantari · York University, Vector Institute, Dalhousie University · WACV

How do you tell an image generator "add glasses, make them a bit younger, but leave everything else alone"? SphereEdit treats each edit as an arrow on a sphere and mixes arrows with an angle.

1 · The problemEdits that leak

Diffusion models make photorealistic images from text, so people want to edit images with them. A good edit should be identity-preserving, fast and controllable. Adding lipstick should not change skin tone, and adding eyeglasses should not reshape the face.

The paper lists what goes wrong with existing approaches:

Optimization / inversion methods are slow, fragile to prompts, and prone to identity drift.

Linear latent operations treat an attribute as a global shift. Edits leak into unrelated regions and don't compose cleanly.

Prompt / attention rewiring relies on layer- or head-specific heuristics with weak spatial grounding, causing "edit bleeding" (lipstick altering cheeks).

The authors' diagnosis is that existing methods capture either global geometry or condition alignment, but not both. SphereEdit tries to do both with no retraining.

2 · The key ideaAttributes are directions; mixing is an angle

For each attribute (say "old"), SphereEdit asks the denoiser twice: once with the attribute text and once with an empty (null) prompt. The difference between the two noise predictions cancels what the two share and leaves what's unique to the attribute. That difference is normalized to length 1, giving a unit vector v̂: "what the model thinks this attribute looks like" at that step.

v_k(t) = ε_θ(z_t, t, c_k) − ε_θ(z_t, t, c_∅) (Eq. 3)
v̂_k = v_k / ‖v_k‖₂

Two geometric properties are claimed. Antipodal symmetry: "old" is approximately the negation of "young", so a negative scale gives a bidirectional edit. Approximate orthogonality: different attributes point in roughly independent directions, so they can be combined cleanly. Mixing weights come from spherical coordinates:

λ₁ = cos θ, λ₂ = sin θ · cos φ, λ₃ = sin θ · sin φ (Eq. 4)
v̂ᵀʳ_k = λ_k · v̂_k (Eq. 5)
Try it: angular mixing

In Figure 1 of the paper, "Old" and "Asian" are the two axes. θ=0 gives attribute a only, θ=π/2 gives b only, and θ=π/4 gives a blend of both. The paper's 1D case is moving along a line (θ=0) with a scale factor controlling intensity. The 2D case is a circle parameterized by θ, and the 3D case is the full sphere with θ and φ.

A single attribute direction scaled by a signed number. The paper's Figure 4 uses scales of −7.5 and +7.5 for "Age", "Female" and "Cyprus".

3 · The methodOne denoising step, five moves

SphereEdit runs inside ordinary Stable Diffusion denoising (DDIM). Nothing is trained or fine-tuned. Click through the stages:

ε̂(t) = ε_base(t) + Σ_k λ_k · v̂_k (Eqs. 8–9, active attributes k)

In plain words: take the model's neutral prediction and add a weighted sum of attribute arrows. The result replaces the original prediction in the DDIM update. A per-step on/off mask m_k(t) lets you schedule edits. For example, "glasses" can be applied only at later steps so the face structure forms first.

Where does the edit apply? Cross-attention masks

Applying a direction everywhere would change irrelevant regions. SphereEdit gathers cross-attention for only the tokens of that attribute's condition, rather than the whole prompt, into a map M_k. "Glasses" lights up around the eyes and "beard" on the lower face. Thresholding at a quantile q gives a region R_k.

What if two edits overlap? Overlap-aware orthogonalization

Two attributes can attend to the same pixels (the paper's example: mouth and beard). Summing them naively entangles them. Orthogonalizing everywhere would distort meaning, so the paper subtracts the projection of one direction onto the other only inside the overlap:

S_jk = 1[(x,y) ∈ R_j ∩ R_k]
v_k ← v_k − ⟨v_k, v_j⟩_{S_jk} · (v_j ⊙ S_jk) (Eqs. 6–7)
Toy demo (illustrative, not from the paper)

Two made-up attention blobs stand in for "mouth" and "beard". A threshold keeps the strongest cells of each. In this toy, q keeps the cells above the q-quantile of the blob's values. The overlap (white) is where orthogonalization would act.

4 · ResultsWhat the experiments show

Setup (as reported): Stable Diffusion v1.5, 50 denoising steps, q = 0.8 unless noted, random seed 0. A three-attribute edit takes about 12 seconds on an NVIDIA L40 GPU. Quantitative tests use the same 100 FFHQ images for every method on two compositional edits. The baselines are SEGA, Composable Diffusion, Cycle Diffusion and TurboEdit.

Qualitatively, the authors report smooth angular interpolation between two attributes without identity drift. They also show edits beyond faces (cars, dogs, cats, art), e.g. truck + cartoon, cat + fox, dog + leopard. They say Composable Diffusion and TurboEdit often alter facial identity or shift global tone, while SEGA and Cycle Diffusion tend to under-edit subtle attributes. For "Glasses+Young+Lipstick", they say TurboEdit introduces artifacts and Composable Diffusion entangles attributes. (These are the authors' visual judgments, shown in their Figures 1 and 3.)

What the metrics mean (per the paper):
  • CLIP-T ↑: does the image reflect the requested attributes (text alignment)?
  • CLIP-I ↑: does the subject remain the same (identity preservation)?
  • DINO ↑: are geometry and layout maintained (structural similarity)?
  • LPIPS ↓: low-level visual difference in texture, color and appearance; lower means fewer artifacts.

5 · AblationsWhat matters

Antipodal control (Fig. 4). With scales of ±7.5, a negative scale inverts or removes the attribute (e.g. old → young, removing wrinkles) and a positive scale accentuates it. The authors report the original identity is preserved and edits stay localized.

Condition-aware masking (Fig. 5). Tested with glasses + lipstick at q = 0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1, where q=0 means an empty mask and q=1 means no mask.

  • Without masking, lipstick spreads beyond the lips and alters skin tone. With masking at q=0.5, edits stay in the attended regions.
  • The text says moderate values (q≈0.5) give well-localized edits, very high values (q>0.8) fragment the region and hurt identity, and q=0.8 is the best compromise between edit strength and precision.
  • Attention for glasses is sharp around the eyes, while lipstick's is weaker and more diffuse, which explains its sensitivity to the threshold.

Note: the paper describes q=0.8 as its default and the best compromise, while also describing q≈0.5 as giving the best localization in the figure. The ablation text isn't fully explicit about how these two fit together.

6 · LimitationsWhat's still open

  • Scale: evaluation covers up to three attributes. The sphere formula extends to more, but it's unclear how interpretable and stable many-way trade-offs would be.
  • Ambiguous opposites: antipodal symmetry works for old/young, but in other domains it's unclear. In medical imaging, is the "opposite" of a disease health or a different condition? For attributes like race, opposites aren't well defined. The authors flag that this raises questions about how spherical geometry interacts with sensitive attributes.

Other things to keep in mind from the text: the quantitative evaluation uses two compositional tasks on 100 FFHQ images, and much of the comparison is visual. The paper says composition uses θ=φ=π/2 "which corresponds to activating all defined attributes", but doesn't spell out how this maps onto Eq. 4 (which at those angles gives λ=(0,0,1)). The implementation details are in the released code (github.com/sala-kon/SphereEdit).

Takeaway

SphereEdit is a training-free recipe: noise-prediction differences become unit directions, cross-attention says where to apply them, local orthogonalization reduces interference, and spherical coefficients set the mix. It gives angular, reversible, composable controls, and the reported results show better identity preservation on most metrics against four baselines.
