1. The problem: accurate, but not trusted

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have performed well at diagnosing brain tumors from MRI scans, non-invasively. Yet they are rarely adopted in clinics. A major reason the paper highlights is lack of explainability: it is unclear which features drive a prediction, so radiologists can't verify that the model is reasoning about the right things.

The paper defines explainability concretely: the ability of a model to focus on the relevant parts of the input when making a prediction. For a brain tumor, that means looking at the tumor rather than at some unrelated region.

The clinical task: pediatric Low-grade Glioma (pLGG)

pLGG is one of the most common brain tumors in children (prevalence rate of 30–40%). Knowing its genetic marker matters for prognosis, risk stratification, and targeted treatment. This paper focuses on distinguishing the two most important markers:

  • BRAF Fusion
  • BRAF V600E Mutation

Today this is mainly determined by biopsy, which is invasive and can be difficult when the tumor is hard to reach. A trustworthy MRI-based predictor could serve as a non-invasive tool.

Why not just give the model expert hints?

Earlier work improved explainability using expert knowledge such as radiologist-annotated semantic features or eye-gaze tracking of where radiologists look. But these are expensive and labor-intensive to collect. Radiology reports, by contrast, already exist for most scans — they are radiologists' expertise written down in their own language.

2. The key idea: learn from scan–report pairs

The method uses contrastive learning (CL), a form of self-supervised learning. No diagnosis labels are needed — only the knowledge of which report belongs to which scan. The model learns to turn each MRI and each report into a vector (an "embedding") such that:

  • a scan and its own report end up close together, and
  • a scan and someone else's report end up far apart.

To succeed, the image model must pick up on the things that reports talk about — which, for a tumor report, is largely the tumor. The authors then reuse this image model for the genetic-marker task.

What's new here, according to the authors

  1. Global and local alignment for 3D MRI. Prior image–report CL work was mostly on 2D chest X-rays; for 3D brain MRI, earlier work aligned only whole-scan and whole-report representations. This paper also aligns fine-grained image patches with report words.
  2. Tumor location as an extra signal. Each case has a tumor location (Supratentorial, Infratentorial, or Transtentorial). The loss treats mismatched pairs differently depending on whether they share a location.
  3. "Semi-hard" negative sampling, a cheap way to choose informative mismatched examples.
  4. Using the learned representations to improve explainability of a downstream diagnosis model, measured against manual tumor segmentations.

5. The global loss, explained (with a calculator)

The global loss uses a triplet loss. For every item in a batch, take it as an anchor (say, an MRI), then pick its positive (its own report) and a negative (a report from another patient). The loss wants the positive to be closer than the negative by at least a margin m. It is done twice: once with MRIs as anchors, once with reports as anchors.

"Distance" here is cosine distance = 1 − cosine similarity: 0 when two vectors point the same way, up to 2 when they point in opposite directions.

Global Loss = Σi=1..2 Σj=1..n max( 0,  d(a,p) − (1−lj)·d(a,n) − c·lj·d(a,n) + m )
where d(x,y) = 1 − cos(x,y)
  • d(a,p): distance from anchor to its true partner — should be small.
  • d(a,n): distance from anchor to the mismatched partner — should be large.
  • lj: 1 if the anchor and negative share the same tumor location, 0 otherwise.
  • c: a coefficient between 0 and 1 applied only when locations match. Tuned to 0.5.
  • m: margin, set to 0.25.
  • max(0, …): once the positive is "close enough" relative to the negative, the loss is zero and nothing more is pushed.

The intuition the authors give: a scan and an unrelated report that describe tumors in the same brain region shouldn't be treated as completely unrelated — their distance should be smaller than that of a mismatched pair with different tumor locations. The coefficient c scales the negative-distance term for same-location pairs.

Reading the formula literally

When l = 1, the negative distance is multiplied by c (e.g. 0.5), so it "counts" less. You can see in the calculator that the loss only hits zero when c·d(a,n) exceeds d(a,p) + m. This is exactly what the paper's Equation 1 computes; the page reports the authors' stated motivation above, and the calculator lets you see the arithmetic for yourself.

Why triplet loss and not the usual contrastive loss? The authors tried both and found triplet loss gave better downstream AUC. They hypothesize that with a small dataset (and small batches), using all negatives in the batch, as standard contrastive loss does, leads to overfitting, whereas one carefully chosen negative per anchor generalizes better.

6. Choosing the negative: random, semi-hard, or hard

Which mismatched example should be the "negative"? Batches have 16 pairs, so each anchor has 15 possible negatives.

  • Random: pick any one. Cheap, but may be too easy to teach much.
  • Hard: compute distance to all 15, pick the closest (most confusing). Effective in general, but costs 15 distance computations per anchor.
  • Semi-hard (proposed): pick 2 at random, keep the closer one. Only 2 distance computations per anchor.

What the paper found: Sampling 2 worked best downstream. With 8 or 15, the authors observed that in many batches the model kept choosing the same representation as the hardest negative for most anchors — which they attribute to small batches lacking diversity and to outliers. Notice in the demo how "Hard" tends to always grab the same nearby point. Full hard negative sampling gave the worst ablation AUC (0.450).

7. The local loss: matching image patches to words

A report sentence like a description of a lesion refers to a specific region of the scan. The local loss tries to make fine-grained image features and the words that describe them line up.

  1. Local image features come from the ResNet's 3rd convolutional module (finer-grained than the last one).
  2. Local text features are the Longformer's output for all tokens except CLS. Since a word can be split into several tokens, their attention scores are summed back into a single word.
  3. Cross-attention (two modules): in one, image features ask questions ("query") of the words ("key/value"); in the other, words query the image. Each produces a weighted version of its query — e.g. "the image patches, re-expressed according to the report."
  4. A triplet loss then pulls each original local representation toward its own weighted counterpart, and away from a mismatched one.
Local Loss = Σk=1..2 Σi=1..2 Σj=1..n max( 0,  d(a,p) − d(a,n) + m )

k: image side or text side. i: whether the anchor is the original or the weighted local representation. Same triplet logic as before, without the location term.

Total loss

Total CL Loss = α × Global Loss + β × Local Loss

α and β are trainable — the model learns how much to weigh each part. Training used AdamW (learning rate 0.0001), batch size 16, on a Tesla V100 GPU. (The main text states 400 epochs; the hyperparameter table in the appendix lists 340 as the optimal value among 230/340/500.)

Keeping a small dataset from overfitting

With only 341 pairs — far fewer than the thousands or millions used in other CL work — the authors:

  • started the image encoder from MedicalNet (Med3D) weights, pretrained on 3D medical images, and the text encoder from Clinical Longformer weights (trained on MIMIC-III);
  • froze the first two ResNet modules and the first ten Longformer layers, fine-tuning only the last two of each (they tried fine-tuning one or two layers; two was best);
  • used dropout (optimal rate 0.25).

Trainable parameters: about 52.1 million in pretraining (31.8M image encoder, 18.9M text encoder, 0.79M cross-attention, 0.53M MLP heads), and about 31.9M in the downstream classifier.

8. The downstream task: predicting the genetic marker

After pretraining, the report encoder is thrown away. Only the trained 3D ResNet is kept, with a fully-connected layer added on top to classify BRAF Fusion vs. BRAF V600E Mutation. No report is needed at prediction time.

Training: cross-entropy loss, AdamW (initial learning rate 0.0001), batch size 16, 20 epochs, with a StepLR scheduler halving the learning rate every 10 epochs. Again, the first two convolutional modules are frozen.

9. Classification results

What do AUC, precision, recall and F1 mean?

The "positive" class here is BRAF V600E Mutation.

  • AUC (the main metric): summarizes the trade-off between true-positive and false-positive rates across all thresholds. 0.5 ≈ coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of the cases called "Mutation", how many really were.
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN): of the real Mutation cases, how many were caught.
  • F1 = 2 × Precision × Recall / (Precision + Recall): a balance of the two.

10. Is the model looking at the tumor?

To measure explainability, the authors take the model's attention heatmap from the self-attention module, normalize it to [0,1], binarize it at a threshold of 0.01 (chosen by visual inspection), and compare it to the radiologists' manual tumor segmentation using the Dice score:

Dice = 2 × |Attention ∩ Tumor| / ( |Attention| + |Tumor| )

Dice is 0 with no overlap and 100% with a perfect match. "3D Dice" uses the whole volume; "2D Dice" uses the single slice with the largest tumor cross-section.

11. Ablations: which ingredients matter?

The authors removed or swapped components and measured downstream results on the external Dataset 2.

  • Both global and local losses are needed: either alone is much worse in AUC (0.608, 0.567).
  • Tumor location helps: dropping it reduces AUC from 0.757 to 0.683.
  • Triplet vs. contrastive loss: triplet wins on AUC (0.757 vs 0.692), but the contrastive-loss version has slightly higher F1 (0.559 vs 0.534).
  • Hard negative sampling hurt badly (AUC 0.450).

12. Limitations and what's next

  • Small dataset. Only 341 image–report pairs for pretraining. The authors say this may limit generalizability, as reflected in the relatively low external results (e.g., external AUC 0.757 vs. 0.877 internal; external recall 0.524).
  • Single institution. Both datasets come from SickKids; an internal dataset was used because the authors found no open-source dataset with MRI, reports, and tumor location together, plus pediatric genetic-marker labels.
  • One downstream task, always with fine-tuning. Tasks needing no fine-tuning (zero- or few-shot classification) weren't explored.
  • Not uniformly better. Internally, the MedicalNet baseline had much higher recall (0.918 vs 0.638); in ablations, contrastive loss had a slightly higher F1.
  • Explainability is measured as overlap with the tumor. Dice values of ~31% (2D) and ~16% (3D) are big improvements over baselines but still far from complete overlap, and depend on a visually chosen binarization threshold (0.01).

Future work proposed by the authors: data augmentation to enlarge the training set, and exploring zero-/few-shot tasks.

Why it matters

The authors argue that the framework (1) needs no human-provided labels for pretraining — just the reports that already exist — which can reduce how much annotated data downstream tasks need; (2) handled data shift from different years and scanners better than baselines; and (3) produced attention maps closer to where radiologists see the tumor, which could help build trust and support radiologists rather than replace them.
