Collaborative Prediction: Tractable Information Aggregation via Agreement

How two parties who each see only part of the picture can reach predictions as good as if they had pooled their data — by trading nothing but their guesses.

Natalie Collina, Ira Globus-Harris, Surbhi Goel, Varun Gupta, Aaron Roth, Mirah Shi · University of Pennsylvania · arXiv:2504.06075 (April 2025)

1. The problem: two halves of the same story

Picture two parties — call them Alice and Bob — who observe different features about the same example and both want to predict the same label. The paper gives several situations where simply pooling the features into one big model is not an option:

Human + AI. A doctor notices patient demeanor, mood, even smell — hard to formalize. A model has learned from vast data the doctor can't use. Neither side's features are legible to the other.
Legal constraints. In regulated industries like healthcare, data may not be shareable for contractual or legal reasons (the motivation for "vertically federated learning").
Bandwidth. The data may be so high-dimensional that sending it all is impractical.
Multi-modal expertise. One party holds CT scans, another holds physician notes. Each has tools for its own modality, none for both together.

The goal: an interaction protocol in which Alice and Bob never share features, only their label predictions, and yet end up with predictions as accurate as the best model in some class defined on the joint feature space.

2. A classic idea — and why it isn't enough

Aumann's agreement theorem (1976) says two perfect Bayesians with a common prior who have common knowledge of each other's beliefs cannot "agree to disagree." Later work (Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis 1982, Aaronson 2005) showed that just exchanging beliefs back and forth makes them agree, quickly.

But agreement does not imply information aggregation. The paper's footnote gives the cleanest example. Click the bits below:

Alice sees xA
Bob sees xB

Label: y = xA + xB mod 2 (XOR), all bits uniform.

Alice's belief P[y=1] = ½   Bob's belief P[y=1] = ½

They agree perfectly… and the truth is 1.

Whatever the bits are, each party alone believes ½, so they "agree" instantly — yet if they pooled their bits they would know y with certainty.

There is also a practical issue: Bayesian agreement results assume both parties share a perfect, common prior and can perform exact Bayes updates — implausible computationally and epistemically. Earlier work by some of the same authors (Collina et al. 2025) replaced Bayesian rationality with tractable calibration conditions, but said nothing about information aggregation. This paper closes that gap.

3. The key idea in one picture

  1. Each day an example arrives. Alice sees xA, Bob sees xB.
  2. They talk for K rounds, alternating. Each round, the speaker announces a single number: their prediction of the label, computed from their own features plus everything said so far.
  3. Each party learns conditionally on what the other just said. Concretely, they make sure their predictions are as good as their best model within each group of days where the other party said roughly the same thing. This is called conversation swap regret.
  4. A "boosting" theorem then says: once they (approximately) agree, the agreed-upon predictions are as good as the best joint model — provided a mild weak learning condition holds.
  5. The final prediction is the last round's. The label is revealed, and both update their learners.
Because only (bounded-precision) predictions are exchanged, communication does not depend on the dimension of the data. And each party only ever runs its own learning algorithm on its own data — the protocol is an efficient reduction from two-party learning to single-party learning.

4. The definitions, in plain language

Write HA for a class of models using only Alice's features (e.g. bounded linear functions of xA), HB similarly for Bob, and HJ for a class on the joint features (e.g. linear functions of (xA, xB)). Labels are in [0,1] and error is squared error. The setting is online and adversarial: no distributional assumptions at all.

Goal: no regret to the joint class

Σt (ŷt − yt)²  ≤  Σt (h(xt) − yt)² + (small)    for every h ∈ HJ

"Over the whole sequence, our total error is not much worse than the best joint model chosen in hindsight" — even though nobody ever sees the joint features. The "small" term should grow slower than T (sublinear), so the average gap vanishes.

Swap regret: being good on every slice of your own predictions

For every value v you predicted:   Σt: ŷt=v (ŷt − yt)²  ≤  minh∈H Σt: ŷt=v (h(xt) − yt)²

Group the days by what you predicted. On each group, no model in your class would have done better. This is stronger than ordinary ("external") regret, which only compares totals over all days.

Conversation swap regret: slice by what the other person said too

For each round k, each bucket of the other party's previous message (round k−1), and each value v of your own message: no model in your class beats you on those days.

This forces each party to genuinely use what they hear. If Bob just said "0.5" and Alice's features can predict the label better on days where Bob says 0.5, Alice must exploit that. The paper shows this implies the "conversation calibration" condition of Collina et al. 2025, which in turn implies fast approximate agreement.

The weak learning condition

On any distribution: if some hJ ∈ HJ beats the best constant by γ, then some hA ∈ HA or hB ∈ HB beats the best constant by at least w(γ).

"Whenever the joint class can find any signal beyond the average, at least one party alone can find some signal." It is "weak" because the individual improvement w(γ) may be far smaller than γ. It generalizes a condition from Globus-Harris et al. (2023) on multicalibration and boosting.

The boosting theorem (Theorem 3.3)

avg error(ŷ) − minhJ avg error(hJ)  ≤  2·w−1( f(T)/T ) + 3·q(T)/T

If one single sequence of predictions has low swap regret to both HA and HB (up to an L1 perturbation q, "distance to swap regret"), and weak learning holds, then that sequence has low regret to the joint class. Intuition from the proof: if the joint class could improve on some slice of the predictions, weak learning says Alice's or Bob's class could also improve on that slice — contradicting swap regret.

Putting it together (Section 4). If both parties have low conversation swap regret, then for large enough K there is some round k where Alice's and Bob's consecutive predictions are close on most days (Theorem 4.1). That round's predictions have low distance to swap regret w.r.t. HA ∪ HB (Theorem 4.2). Error cannot increase much in later rounds, so the final round inherits the joint-class guarantee (Theorem 4.3). Notably, the protocol keeps talking for all K rounds even after agreement, so the guarantee applies to a single well-defined round.

This sandbox implements a simplified version of the paper's batch algorithm (Section 6, COLLABORATE + CROSS-BOOST) with 1-D linear regression as each party's learner. It omits the inner INTERNAL-BOOST loop. The paper itself contains no experiments — this is purely an illustration of the mechanism on toy data, including two constructions taken from the paper.

One example's conversation
How each round works here

Round 0: Bob fits a line to y using xB and announces rounded predictions (multiples of 1/m). In each later round the speaker groups the examples by the value the other party just announced (a level set). On each group they fit their own line using only their own feature. If it beats simply repeating the other's value by more than 1/m², they use it; otherwise they "agree" with the other's value on that group. The process stops when a round changes nothing — exactly the halting rule of COLLABORATE.

6. When does weak learning hold? (and how weak is it?)

Theorem 3.6. If Alice's and Bob's classes are bounded (|f| ≤ C, C ≥ ½), star-shaped (closed under shrinking), include intercepts, and the joint class is their Minkowski sum (hJ(x) = hA(xA) + hB(xB)), then weak learning holds with

w(γ) = γ² / (16 C²)

In particular, norm-bounded linear functions on each side satisfy it relative to bounded linear functions on the joint space. Proof idea: a joint predictor that helps must be correlated with the label, so one of its two halves is correlated with the label; a suitably scaled-down copy of that half gives a (quadratically smaller) gain.

Is the square necessary? Theorem 3.8 says yes. Its construction: xA = ξA/2, xB = xA + ξB/(2ρ), y = ξB, with random signs ξ. The difference xB − xA cancels the noise, but a bounded joint predictor can only use a scaled-down version. Drag ρ:

The joint gain shrinks like 1/ρ while the best individual gain shrinks like 1/ρ², so no w(γ) growing faster than γ² can work. With unbounded weights (Theorem 3.7) the joint predictor achieves a gain of 1 while Bob's gain 1/(ρ²+1) can be made arbitrarily small — so boundedness is necessary too. And Theorem A.1 shows multiplicative joint classes (hA·hB) can fail weak learning entirely: with y = xAxB on random signs, neither side alone has any signal.

7. What the paper proves

Online, adversarial, real-valued labels

  • Efficient reductions. Any online learner with external-regret guarantees for H can be turned into one with swap regret (Garg et al. 2024), and the paper turns that into one with conversation swap regret (Algorithm 2: run a separate copy for each round and each bucket of the other party's last message).
  • End-to-end (Theorem 4.6). If HA, HB have efficient sublinear-regret online learners and satisfy weak learning w.r.t. HJ, and the conversation length K grows (not constant) but sublinearly, then the final predictions have o(T) regret to the best joint model.
  • Linear case (Theorem 4.9). Using the Azoury–Warmuth online linear regression algorithm, with K = T3/28 the regret to the best bounded joint linear model is Õ(T55/56·√(max(C²,C)·d·log(T15/56/ρ))) with probability 1−ρ.
  • Tradeoff (Remark 4.7). Longer conversations improve the bound but cost more daily communication and computation.

High-dimensional outcomes: talk in actions

When outcomes live in [0,1]d, asking for swap regret on the predictions themselves would inherit exponential dependence on d. Instead, each party keeps an internal prediction but only announces its best-response action BRu(ŷ) = argmaxa u(a, ŷ) for a known utility u (linear and Lipschitz in the outcome — e.g. any expected-utility maximizer over d states).

  • Parties are required to be decision calibrated and decision cross-calibrated (from Zhao et al., Noarov et al., Lu et al.) conditional on the other's last action.
  • A decision version of weak learning and boosting (Theorem 5.16) applies.
  • Theorem 5.21: with K = ω(1) and K = o(√T), the final actions have o(T) regret to the best joint policy. Bounds and communication depend only polynomially on d and the number of actions |A|.

Batch (i.i.d.) setting: a simpler algorithm

A two-party generalization of "level-set boosting" (Globus-Harris et al. 2023), using only a squared-error regression oracle for each party's own class — the algorithm the demo above simplifies.

  • Convergence (Theorem 6.10): COLLABORATE halts within R = m² rounds; each inner boosting run within m² sub-rounds (squared error drops by ≥ 1/m² per round).
  • In-sample accuracy (Theorem 6.11): excess error over the best joint model ≤ 2·w−1(3/m).
  • Generalization (Theorem 6.12): holds once n ≥ O((m7 d log(md) + log(1/δ))/ε²), with d the pseudodimension of HJ.
  • Test-time compute: to predict a new example, the parties replay a polynomial-length conversation using their stored model transcripts.

Lifting to one-shot Bayesian agreement

Perfect Bayesians with a correct common prior are unbiased conditional on any event, so they automatically have (near) zero expected conversation swap regret. Imagining an arbitrarily long i.i.d. sequence and using permutation invariance, the online theorems transfer to a single conversation.

  • Theorem 7.7: after K rounds, expected excess error vs. the best model in HJ is O(w−1(K−1/3)), for classes with finite fat-shattering dimension satisfying weak learning.
  • Linear (Remark 7.8): O(C·K−1/6) for bounded linear classes — for any prior.
  • The authors describe this as the first distribution-agnostic information aggregation theorem in the agreement literature: unlike Frongillo et al. (2023), no assumption ("information substitutes") on the prior is needed.

8. Why each ingredient is needed (Section 8)

Theorem 8.1. Take xA = ξA, xB = ξA + ξB, y = ξB (random signs). Alice's best linear predictor is the constant 0 (error 1), Bob's is xB/2 (error 1/4), and the joint linear predictor xB − xA is perfect (error 0). Since Alice's optimal predictor is constant, no function of the two optimal individual predictions can beat 1/4. "Late fusion" fails; the demo's first dataset shows interaction recovers the signal.

This also circumvents a "no-free-lunch" barrier for non-interactive human-AI aggregation (Peng, Garg, Kleinberg 2024).

Theorem 8.2. If a triple (HA, HB, HJ) fails weak learning, there's a distribution where the joint class beats the constant but neither individual class does. Then always predicting the best constant has zero swap regret to both individual classes — yet positive regret to the joint class. So weak learning is not just sufficient for the boosting step but necessary.

Theorem 8.6. Bits xA, xB ∈ {0,1} uniform, y = xA·xB. Consider predicting ŷ = xA/2.

PredictorExpected squared error
ŷ = xA/2 (our sequence)1/8
Best linear in xA: xA/21/8
Best linear in xB: xB/21/8
Best joint linear: (xA+xB)/2 − 1/41/16

The sequence has zero external regret to both individual classes but regret 1/16 to the joint class — even though linear classes satisfy weak learning. (Swap regret catches this: on the days where ŷ = ½, i.e. xA = 1, the label equals xB, which Bob's class predicts exactly.)

Frongillo et al. (2023) proved agreement implies Bayes-optimality under an information substitutes condition (diminishing marginal value of information). The paper shows its weak learning condition is strictly weaker: information substitutes implies weak learning with w(γ) = γ/2 (Lemma 8.4), but bounded linear functions on [−1,1] satisfy weak learning (with w = Θ(γ²)) while violating information substitutes (Corollary 8.5).

9. Limitations and open questions

  • Honest participants. The protocols assume both parties follow them faithfully. The authors flag strategic manipulation — e.g. a capable AI deceptively steering outcomes — as a crucial open direction requiring strategy-proof designs.
  • Which classes satisfy weak learning? The positive results cover bounded, star-shaped classes with a Minkowski-sum joint class (linear-like). Boundedness is necessary, and multiplicative interactions can break the condition. Extending beyond this structure is open.
  • Worst-case rates. Guarantees are against adversarial sequences, and the rates are slow (e.g. T55/56 for linear functions; K−1/6 in the Bayesian linear case). Beyond-worst-case models (smoothed analysis, selective prediction) might yield tighter bounds and fewer rounds.
  • No empirical evaluation yet. The paper is theoretical; the authors suggest multi-modal learning as a first testbed for scalability, performance, and communication efficiency.
  • Squared error / linear utilities. The real-valued results use squared error; the decision extension assumes utilities linear and Lipschitz in the outcome.
Bottom line. Simple exchanges of predictions — no features, no cryptography, no common prior — can provably reach the accuracy of the best joint model in a rich class, as long as each party learns conditionally on what it hears and a mild weak learning condition holds. Interaction, weak learning, and swap-style regret are each shown to be essential.
