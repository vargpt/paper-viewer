SphereEdit: Spherical Semantic Editing in Diffusion Models

Salamata Konate, Hassan Hamidi, Elham Dolatabadi, Frank Rudzicz, Laleh Seyyed-Kalantri · York University, Vector Institute, Dalhousie University, CIFAR

SphereEdit edits images with a pretrained diffusion model, without any training. Each attribute you ask for (glasses, lipstick, age) becomes a unit-length direction. Angles on a sphere decide how much of each attribute to apply, and the model's own cross-attention decides where in the image it goes.

Stable Diffusion v1.5 50 DDIM steps ~12 s for 3 attributes on one L40 No fine-tuning
The problem

Edits that leak, drift, and don't combine

Adding lipstick should change the lips and nothing else. In practice, existing editors fall short in three ways. Optimization and inversion methods are slow and let the person's identity drift. Linear latent edits treat an attribute as a global shift, so the change bleeds into unrelated regions and two edits don't stack cleanly. Attention-rewiring methods depend on hand-picked layers and heads, so "lipstick" can end up tinting the cheeks.

The authors set four requirements for a better editor:

Localized

Only the regions relevant to the attribute change.

Composable

Several attributes combine predictably.

Stable

Bounded magnitude, so nothing over-saturates.

Training-free

The diffusion model is used as is.

Core idea

Attributes are directions on a unit sphere

At every denoising step, the UNet is run twice: once with the attribute prompt ck and once with an empty prompt c∅. The difference between the two noise predictions is what the model thinks the attribute looks like at this step:

vk(t) = εθ(zt, t, ck) − εθ(zt, t, c∅) → v̂k = vk / ‖vk‖2

Normalizing removes scale differences between attributes. What remains is a direction, and directions live on a sphere. Spherical coordinates then give each attribute a weight λk. The weights always lie on the unit sphere, so the total edit stays bounded however you mix them:

λ1 = cos θ, λ2 = sin θ cos φ, λ3 = sin θ sin φ Δεt = Σ λk v̂k

The same formula covers one, two or three attributes. Try each case below.

    Method · steps 2 and 3
    
Where to edit: thresholding cross-attention

  

  

    
Cross-attention shows which pixels each prompt token attends to. SphereEdit builds a separate map Mk for each attribute's own tokens and keeps the pixels above the quantile q, giving a region Rk. Where two regions overlap (Sjk = Rj ∩ Rk), the two directions could interfere, so they are decorrelated there and only there.

    
The face below is a simplified illustration. Glasses attention is sharp around the eyes, and lipstick attention is weaker and more diffuse, as the paper observes in Fig. 5. Move q and watch the regions and their overlap.

Illustrative maps, not model output. A higher q keeps only the most strongly attended pixels. In the paper, low thresholds let lipstick spill onto the skin, very high ones fragment the target, and q = 0.8 is the default.

  


  

    
Inside the overlap, the second direction loses its component along the first, using a masked Gram–Schmidt step:

  

  
vk ← vk − ⟨vk, vj⟩Sjk (vj ⊙ Sjk)

  

    

      
    

    

      
      

        
        
      

      

    

    
Orthogonalizing everywhere would distort what each attribute means. Restricting it to Sjk removes interference only where both edits compete for the same pixels.

The qualitative comparisons (Fig. 3) point the same way. Composable Diffusion and TurboEdit tend to change facial identity and shift the global tone. Beyond faces, the same procedure edits cars (Truck + Cartoon), dogs (Cat + Fox), cats (Dog + Leopard) and artwork (Sketch + Cubism).

  






  

    Open questions
    
Limitations

  

  

    
Beyond three attributes
The experiments use at most three attributes. The formula extends to more dimensions, but it is not yet known whether many-way trade-offs stay interpretable and stable.

    
What is the opposite?
"Old" versus "young" works naturally. In medical imaging, the opposite of a disease could be health or another condition. For demographic attributes such as race, opposites are not well defined. This matters for how the geometry is applied to sensitive attributes.
