SphereEdit

01The problem: edits that leak

Diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion can make photorealistic images from text. Users often want to edit an existing image instead, for example by adding glasses, making someone look older, or turning a truck into a cartoon. Such an edit should be:

Identity-preserving
Adding lipstick should not change skin tone. Adding eyeglasses should not reshape the face.
Fast & controllable
No slow per-image optimization, and no trial-and-error prompt tinkering.

The paper groups existing approaches and the problems each one has:

  • Optimization and inversion methods are slow, sensitive to the exact prompt, and prone to identity drift.
  • Linear latent operations treat an attribute as a global shift. Edits leak into unrelated regions and don't combine cleanly.
  • Prompt- and attention-rewiring methods rely on layer- or head-specific heuristics with weak spatial grounding. This causes edit bleeding, for example "lipstick" altering the cheeks.
The authors' diagnosis: existing methods capture either the global geometry of the model's representations or where the text condition applies in the image, but not both.

02The key idea: attributes are directions on a sphere

SphereEdit treats each semantic attribute ("glasses", "old", "cartoon"…) as a unit-length arrow (a direction) in the space of the denoiser's predictions. Since every arrow has length 1, all of them point to locations on a unit sphere. This viewpoint brings three useful properties:

↔ Antipodal symmetry
The opposite direction gives roughly the opposite meaning: "old" is approximately the negation of "young". One arrow therefore supports both adding and removing an attribute.
⊥ Approximate orthogonality
Different attributes point in roughly perpendicular directions, so they can be combined without interfering much.
◐ Angular mixing
Rotating an angle trades one attribute against another smoothly, with bounded overall strength.
⌖ Attention-based localization
Cross-attention maps show where each attribute belongs (glasses near the eyes, beard on the lower face), so each edit stays in its region.

The whole procedure runs at inference time. Nothing is retrained or fine-tuned, so it is plug-and-play.

03The method, step by step

Image diffusion works by repeatedly denoising. At each step a network (a UNet) predicts the noise in the current noisy image, and a scheduler removes some of it. SphereEdit works inside every single denoising step. Click through the five stages (from Figure 2 of the paper):

Background: the diffusion setup

The image is first compressed into a smaller "latent" representation z0 by a pretrained autoencoder. Noise is added according to a schedule:

zt = √ᾱt · z0 + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε, ε ~ N(0, I)

In plain words: at step t, the latent is a blend of the clean image and random noise. As t grows, the blend contains more noise and less image.

ε̂ = εθ(zt, t, c)

The UNet εθ looks at the noisy latent, the timestep, and a text embedding c, and guesses the noise. SphereEdit first uses DDIM inversion to recover the noisy trajectory of a real input image. It then modifies this noise guess during denoising, which steers the result toward the edit.

Step 1 in detail: what does the model think "glasses" looks like?

vk(t) = εθ(zt, t, ck) − εθ(zt, t, c∅) then v̂k = vk / ‖vk‖

The UNet runs twice on the same noisy latent: once with the attribute text (e.g. "glasses") and once with an empty/null prompt ∅. Everything they share cancels in the subtraction. What remains is the attribute's own contribution. The result is then normalized to length 1, so a "loud" attribute doesn't overpower a "quiet" one.

A binary on/off schedule mk(t) can also switch each attribute on only during certain steps. For example, "glasses" can be applied only at later steps, so the facial structure is reconstructed first.

Steps 4–5 in detail: mixing and injecting

λ1 = cos θ, λ2 = sin θ cos φ, λ3 = sin θ sin φ
Δεt = Σk active λk v̂k ⟹ ε̂(t) = εbase(t) + Δεt

The two angles θ and φ set how much of each attribute to apply. These are the standard formulas for converting spherical coordinates into x, y, z. The weighted directions are summed and added to the ordinary noise prediction. The modified prediction then replaces the original in the DDIM update. An extra scale factor (0 to 1, or larger for single-attribute edits) can adjust how visible the edit is.

04Try it: angular control

With two attributes, the weights are (λa, λb) = (cos θ, sin θ), which is a point on a circle. Drag the dot or use the slider. At θ = 0° you get pure attribute a. At 90° you get pure b. At 45° you get an equal blend. At 180° you get the opposite of a. The paper reports that in its Figure 1 this works even though only the "old" embedding was provided.

Three attributes: the full sphere

With three attributes, a second angle φ is added and the point (λ1, λ2, λ3) moves over a sphere. Because λ1² + λ2² + λ3² = 1 always holds, the total edit strength stays bounded. This addresses the paper's "stable, no over-saturation" criterion.

λ1 glasses
λ2 lipstick
λ3 young

Sum of squares: (always 1). A negative λ pushes the image away from that attribute.

05Try it: keeping edits in their place

If the edit direction were applied to the whole image, lipstick might tint the skin. To prevent this, SphereEdit uses each attribute's own cross-attention map Mk, which shows where in the image the model "looks" when it reads that specific word. The map is thresholded at a quantile q to produce a region Rk, and the edit is confined there.

Toy illustration (synthetic attention maps)

In this toy, a pixel is kept if its attention is above the q-th quantile of that map. ■ glasses ■ lipstick ■ beard ■ overlap.

What the paper found (Figure 5): with broad masks, edits leak (lipstick spills onto skin, global tone shifts). Moderate thresholds (q ≈ 0.5) keep glasses on the frames and lipstick on the lips. Thresholds that are too aggressive (q > 0.8) fragment the target region and hurt identity preservation. The attention for "glasses" is sharp around the eyes, while "lipstick" is weaker and more diffuse, which makes it more sensitive to the threshold. The authors use q = 0.8 as the best compromise between edit strength and spatial precision.
Note: the paper's Figure 5 caption describes the endpoints as "q = 0 means empty mask, q = 1 means no mask". The toy above follows the main text's description, in which a higher threshold gives a tighter mask.

06Try it: disentangling overlapping attributes

Sometimes two attributes claim the same pixels, for example "mustache" and "lipstick" around the mouth. Simply adding their directions there can tangle them together. Making all directions perpendicular everywhere (global orthogonalization) would distort their meaning. SphereEdit instead removes the shared component only inside the overlap:

Sjk = 1[(x,y) ∈ Rj ∩ Rk] vk ← vk − ⟨vk, vj⟩Sjk (vj ⊙ Sjk)

In plain words: find the pixels where both attributes are active. Within those pixels only, measure how much of vk points along vj (a masked inner product) and subtract that part. This is one Gram–Schmidt step restricted to the overlap. Outside the overlap, both directions stay unchanged.

07The algorithm in one box

For one denoising step t:
1 εbase ← UNet(zt, t, ∅)
2 for each active attribute k:
3 vk ← UNet(zt, t, ck) − εbase
4 v̂k ← vk / ‖vk‖
5 Mk ← CrossAttention(zt, ck)
6 for each pair (j,k): Sjk ← Overlap(Mj, Mk); vk ← vk − ⟨vk,vj⟩S(vj⊙Sjk)
7 λk ← SphericalCoefficients(θ, φ)
8 ε̂ ← εbase + Σ λk v̂k
9 zt−1 ← DDIM update using ε̂

08Results

What the images show (qualitative)

  • Angular traversal (Figure 1, age × race): rotating θ produces a continuous interpolation, and intermediate angles give blends without identity drift.
  • Beyond faces: Truck + Cartoon, Cat + Fox, Dog + Leopard, Sketch + Cubism. The truck keeps its structure when cartoonized, the dog keeps its shape when gaining fox-like fur, and the cat smoothly acquires leopard textures.
  • Versus baselines (Figure 3: Glasses, Glasses+Young, Glasses+Young+Lipstick): according to the authors, Composable Diffusion and TurboEdit often alter facial identity and introduce global tone shifts, SEGA and Cycle-Diffusion tend to under-edit subtle attributes, TurboEdit introduces artifacts, and Composable Diffusion entangles attributes. SphereEdit keeps edits in the intended regions.

Numbers (quantitative)

The evaluation uses 100 FFHQ face images and two compositional edits. The metrics are:

  • CLIP-T ↑: does the image match the requested text?
  • CLIP-I ↑: is the subject still the same person?
  • DINO ↑: are the structure and layout preserved?
  • LPIPS ↓: low-level visual difference (lower means fewer artifacts).

Green = best in column. On Glasses + Mustache, SphereEdit leads CLIP-T, CLIP-I and DINO, and is just behind SEGA on LPIPS. On Eyebrow + Lipstick, it leads CLIP-I, DINO and LPIPS, while TurboEdit edges it on CLIP-T by 0.0012.

09Ablations: which parts matter?

Antipodal control (Figure 4)

The paper scales the directions for "Age", "Female", and "Cyprus" (a race/ethnicity condition) by −7.5 and +7.5. The negative scale removes or inverts the attribute: decreasing Age removes wrinkles and softens features. The positive scale amplifies it: increasing Age adds age-related texture. The authors report that identity is preserved and edits stay localized, so moving in opposite directions along one arrow gives interpretable, reversible changes.

0 → original image.

Condition-aware masking (Figure 5)

Without masking, lipstick spreads beyond the lips and changes skin tone. With masking it stays on the attended regions. The threshold q controls the trade-off between leakage (masks too broad) and fragmentation (masks too tight). The authors conclude that masking is critical for disentangled multi-attribute edits. Use the interactive mask demo in section 05 to explore this trade-off.

10Limitations (as stated by the authors)

  • Only up to three attributes were tested. The spherical formulas extend to more dimensions, but it is not yet clear how interpretable and stable many-way trade-offs would be in practice.
  • "Opposite" isn't always meaningful. Antipodal symmetry emerges consistently in the embedding space, but its meaning can be ambiguous. In medical imaging, is the opposite of a disease health, or a different condition? For demographic attributes such as race, opposites are not well defined. This raises important questions about how spherical geometry interacts with sensitive attributes.
The quantitative evaluation covers two compositional face edits on 100 FFHQ images. The results for animals, vehicles, and art are shown qualitatively.

11Takeaway

SphereEdit represents each attribute as a unit-length direction in the denoiser's prediction space. It uses antipodal symmetry for adding or removing an attribute, cross-attention masks to keep edits in place, overlap-only orthogonalization to keep attributes from tangling, and spherical angles to mix several attributes with bounded strength. The result is a training-free, plug-and-play editor whose controls are simple angles. Code: github.com/sala-kon/SphereEdit.
