01The problem: photos through glass

Taking pictures through glass is hard. Light bounces off the glass and adds itself on top of the subject you actually wanted. Photos from cars and airplanes show the cabin, photos from buildings include ceiling lights, paintings are covered by haze, and window-shopping shots get photobombed by the photographer.

Removing those reflections is difficult because they happen everywhere — shops, cars, planes, museums, eyeglasses, screens — and they depend on the time of day, the lighting, the scene and its textures.

Why not just collect real training data? One way to get a "true" answer is to take a second photo with black material behind the glass so only the reflection reaches the camera, then subtract. This works only if nothing moves and the lighting doesn't change — which severely restricts how much data you can collect. Placing glass panes in a scene is another option, but then the lighting on both sides is usually similar, which is unrealistic.

What a practical system must do

The authors target consumer photography, with six requirements:

  1. Handle the typical reflections in consumer photos.
  2. Minimize user interactions (steps, taps, strokes).
  3. Allow photo capture in a typical amount of time.
  4. Show results on screen for review in about 5 seconds.
  5. Produce results at the input image's full resolution.
  6. Make editing easy, for error correction and aesthetics.

02The key idea in one paragraph

Instead of training on hard-to-collect real reflection photos, the authors simulate millions of reflections by physically combining pairs of ordinary RAW photos — images whose pixel values are still proportional to actual light. Because the mixing respects real physics (light adds, glass attenuates by angle, different light sources have different colors and strengths), a model trained only on these simulations works on real photos without any fine-tuning on real data.

The four contributions

  • A way to synthesize training data so that models don't need fine-tuning on real images.
  • Showing that training and testing on RAW improves performance significantly — more than the architectural differences between prior methods.
  • Using a contextual photo to help identify what is reflection.
  • An upsampler that greatly reduces artifacts, producing 1K previews in ~5 s and full-resolution output.

03Light simply adds — but only in the right units

The most fundamental principle in the paper is that glass superimposes two scenes. Photons from the scene behind the glass (the transmission, t) and photons bounced from the scene in front of it (the reflection, r) land on the same sensor pixels and are counted together:

m = t + r

Here m is the "mixture" — the photo you actually take. This equation is only true for linear, scene-referred images (like RAW), where a pixel value is proportional to how much light arrived. Ordinary JPEGs have been tone-mapped, white-balanced, sharpened and compressed, so adding two JPEGs does not reproduce what real glass does.

What to notice (these effects are described in the paper's Fig. 3 and discussion):

  • Strong in shadows, transparent in highlights. In linear mixing, a dim reflection is easily visible over dark regions but nearly vanishes over the bright sky. Prior works that blend 8-bit tone-mapped images get this wrong: their reflections overpower highlights and are too weak in shadows, which tone mapping has boosted.
  • Indoor reflections are mostly lights. Indoor light is weak, so reflections of interiors over outdoor scenes are typically regions near light sources or windows — and they often look yellow because of the indoor illuminant color.
  • Illuminant color must mix before white balancing. The two sides of the glass are usually lit by different light sources. If you white-balance each image separately, the warm cast of the indoor reflection disappears — not what a camera actually records.

04Where in the camera pipeline should you mix?

A RAW file becomes a finished photo through a series of steps, described by Adobe Camera RAW (ACR) and the DNG specification. Click each step. The authors needed a stage that is linear, keeps the illuminant color, and is sensor-independent, so photos from different cameras can be summed.

Answer: Step 4, XYZ color. It is linear with respect to light, sensor-independent, and the illuminant color is still present. Reflections are simulated there; models then operate on linear RGB (Step 6), and outputs are written back as new RAW images by inverting steps 3–6.

A technical subtlety: ACR offers two white-balancing paths. The common one (white balance, then convert to XYZ with a "forward matrix") is the wrong order for simulation. The authors use the second path via "color matrices", which converts to XYZ before white balancing. Every DNG file is required to include these color matrices.

05Glass physics: Fresnel, exposure, blur and ghosts

Mixing correctly isn't just about adding. The simulation models how much light the glass reflects, how bright each scene is, camera geometry, defocus and double reflections.

Fresnel attenuation — the most essential effect

Glass reflects only a fraction α of incoming light, and that fraction depends on the angle θi at which light hits the glass:

α = ½ (α⊥ + α∥),   α⊥ = sin²(θi−θ′i) / sin²(θi+θ′i),   α∥ = tan²(θi−θ′i) / tan²(θi+θ′i)

In plain words: light is made of two polarizations, each reflecting a different amount; α is their average. θ′i = arcsin(sin θi / κ) is the angle the light bends to inside glass, where κ is the glass's refractive index. Looking straight at glass, only a few percent reflects; at grazing angles much more does. The transmitted scene is dimmed by (1 − α), which is usually close to 1.

Reflection, in photographic stops
Transmitted fraction 1 − α
Ghost (2nd reflection) β = (1−α)·α·(1−α)

The paper samples κ uniformly in [1.47, 1.53]. It reports that for θi between 0° and 45°, Fresnel attenuation accounts for up to −4 stops, gradually strengthening to −1 stop for rays that strike the glass at 83°.

Because θi changes across the image when you shoot obliquely, reflections are stronger toward the edges of a photo. This creates natural spatial "priors" — for example, skies typically reflect near the top of images.

Exposure: un-expose, mix, re-expose

Each source photo was taken with its own exposure e = s · g / n² (shutter speed s, ISO gain g, aperture n). To put both scenes on the same physical scale, each image is divided by its own exposure: t′ = t / et, r′ = r / er. Now pixels are proportional to true scene brightness, so a sunny street really is much brighter than a lamp-lit room. Then a virtual camera "captures" the mixture:

m = C(m′) = W · e′ · m′,   e′ = τ / E[m′]

In plain words: pick a new exposure e′ so that the average pixel lands at a target value τ (sRGB 0.4), then apply a new white balance W (a 3×3 color matrix) chosen by an automatic white-balancer (C5). If any pixels in t or r are saturated, e′ = 1 / min(max t′, max r′) so they stay saturated. The same white balance is applied to m, t, r and the context photo.

Geometry, defocus and double reflections

Camera pose

Camera azimuth to the glass up to 50°, field of view 50°–80°. Pairs are rejected if their tilt differs by more than 15°, or either is tilted beyond 45° or rolled beyond 10° — which aligns horizons of t and r.

Defocus blur

Circle of confusion δ = |do−df| f² / (do N (df−f)), using iPhone-like optics (N≈1.6, f≈26mm equiv.) and distances 1–100 ft. Reflections typically turn out sharp — consistent with prior observations.

Double reflection

A ghost from the glass's back surface: jd = α·j + β·jw, with β = (1−α)α(1−α). Glass thickness 8–20 mm, viewing distance 0.5–2 m, 50% chance of a double pane.

Deliberately not simulated: global color casts, dirt and scratches on the glass — existing editing tools can correct those.

06The optional "context" photo

A single photo is ambiguous: is that colorful shape part of the shop window display, or a reflection of something behind you? The system optionally accepts a contextual photo c — any picture of the scene behind the photographer. It can come from the selfie camera at the same time, or by quickly turning around. It doesn't need to be simultaneous or aligned.

The authors note three reasons the context photo rarely matches the reflection exactly:

  1. Even if the cameras sit together, the reflection's viewpoint is shifted by twice the distance to the glass.
  2. If you shoot the glass at an angle, turning 180° gives a view with little common content.
  3. With the selfie camera, the photographer may block part of the reflected scene.
Clever training trick: rather than burden users, the context photo is defined as any view of the reflected scene that does not show the same parts as the reflection. In simulation, each reflection source image is split into two non-overlapping halves (left/right or top/bottom): one half becomes the reflection r, the other becomes the context c. This prevents the model from "cheating" by matching identical patches, while still letting c carry lighting color and scene type (indoor, outdoor, city, nature…).

07Building millions of realistic examples

Not every physically correct mixture is useful. Some are overexposed, some reflections are invisible, and some are so strong nobody could tell what the subject is. So the authors search a huge space of simulated mixtures and keep only well-exposed, well-mixed ones.

Recipe (Function 1)

  1. Take a random pair of XYZ images (i, j).
  2. Split j into non-overlapping reflection and context parts (r, c).
  3. Pick a transmission part t from i.
  4. Un-expose t and r using their exposure metadata.
  5. Apply the geometric simulation (Fresnel, pose, blur, ghosts).
  6. Composite m = t + r.
  7. Compute a new exposure and white balance for m.
  8. Apply the same white balance to t, r, c.
  9. Convert everything to linear sRGB.

Quality filters

Well exposed: mean pixel value within a normal distribution of the RAW dataset.

Well mixed: similarity (SSIM) between m and t must be within [0.4, 0.94]. Too high → the reflection is imperceptible. Too low → not interpretable even by a human.

Not spread thin: the spatial standard deviation of SSIM must be ≥ 0.05, removing faint reflections spread evenly across the frame.

Pairings: outdoor×indoor, indoor×outdoor, indoor×indoor. Outdoor×outdoor is omitted as uncommon.

A striking qualitative check (Fig. 3): real reflection photos were shuffled together with simulated ones, and for each real photo a similar synthetic example could be found in the dataset — because it exceeds a million examples.

Why mask-based upsampling avoids artifacts

09Results

Public reflection benchmarks only provide JPG/PNG images, not RAW. So numbers are reported on the simulated RAW test sets, and visual results use RAW photos captured in the field (some with captured ground truth). Quality is measured with PSNR (pixel accuracy, higher is better) and SSIM (structural similarity, shown as %, higher is better). "Control" means doing nothing — comparing the captured photo m directly to the true t or r.

Reading "%↑": this column measures SSIM improvement relative to the control. In the table values, it equals the fraction of the gap between the untouched photo and a perfect score that the method closed — e.g. control SSIMt 85.8 → 95.7 closes about 70% of the remaining 14.2 points. Negative means the method made the image worse than doing nothing.

Finding 1: RAW training matters more than architecture

Prior methods were retrained on the authors' RAW data and compared against their published 8-bit weights (run on an 8-bit version of the test set).

Retraining DSRNet, Zhu and Zhang on RAW improves them by ≈40 percentage points SSIMt, while the differences among them are only ≈20 pp. The authors' own models (2.5M parameters, versus 125M for DSRNet) score best: Ours+ctx reaches PSNRt 33.23 dB and SSIMt 95.7%.

Feeding a random context photo is significantly worse than the real one (p < 1.7e-11), so the model uses the content of c rather than just extra capacity. Giving it the exact reflection as context at test time doesn't help further — the context doesn't need to match the reflection, because the model was trained with disjoint crops. Visually, the context helps e.g. produce more correct, uniform color on an Egyptian tablet (Fig. 1).

Finding 4: the upsampler beats prior upsampling

Upsampling from 256p to 2048p, either from ground-truth low-res images (GT) or from the base model's actual outputs (end-to-end, E2E).

The authors' upsampler reaches 47.77 dB PSNRT on GT inputs versus 42.24 for V-DESIRR with cycle-consistency. Removing the masking operations ("Ours-NM") drops it almost to V-DESIRR's level, showing masking is the key ingredient. Generic super-resolution models (SUPIR, Real-ESRGAN) score below even the "do nothing" control on SSIM. End-to-end: 30.62 vs 30.27 dB PSNRT. The upsampler uses 29% fewer parameters than V-DESIRR.

Real photos and bonus abilities

  • In the wild: museums, nature, shopping, a midday city, artwork — from cameras not used to build the training data. Published 8-bit models don't consistently remove these reflections; retrained on RAW they improve significantly, but still add blur and colored artifacts.
  • Subtle reflections: on museum pieces (Rembrandt's "St. Matthew and the Angel", Ansel Adams' "Residents of Hornitos") the model recovers reflections hard to see with the naked eye — even revealing the photographer holding a phone. This is helped by RAW's bit depth (≥15-bit test images; 12- and 14-bit sources).
  • Lens flares: the model can sometimes remove them, although no lens flares are in the training data.
  • Editing: because both t and r are output, a photographer can recolor, rearrange, or partially restore the reflection (done in Photoshop with "Linear Dodge (Add)").

10Limitations and open problems

Saturation → hole filling. When t or r is so bright it pushes the other into the noise floor or clips it, the hidden content is gone; the problem becomes inpainting. Light sources can be missed or leave halos.
Overlapping textures. When textured regions of t and r overlap (e.g. a stone wall reflected over a dress) errors occur. Color differences help; without them the model must repair or hallucinate.
Rare lighting. Results are less consistent for interiors flooded with very strong light, and for nighttime scenes where indoor lights reflect over dark cityscapes — both rare in training data. Night-time source images could help.
Upsampling new detail. High-frequency reflection textures invisible at 256p can be copied into the full-res transmission, since the low-res outputs can't guide them.
8-bit inputs. Models are trained for RAW. On JPEGs (with gamma pseudo-inverted) removal works somewhat but is often incomplete, because camera pipelines apply diverse non-linear finishing. Testing on 8-bit benchmarks is not meaningful for this system.
Scope & simplifications. Works best when glass covers the whole view; local reflections (shiny cars, distant windows) are left alone — usually desirable. Saturated sky in a context photo can cause errors. The double-reflection model assumes perfectly parallel panes. Unusual pairings (bathrooms reflected on beaches) remain in the data.
Privacy note from the authors: because the model can uncover reflections that are invisible to the eye, privacy should be protected.

✓Takeaway

Simulate reflections the way physics does — in linear RAW units, with correct illuminant colors, brightness, Fresnel attenuation and geometry — and search millions of such simulations for realistic cases. Models trained only on this data generalize to real photos, and the choice of training data matters more than the choice of network. Add an optional look-behind photo and a mask-based upsampler, and you get a practical system that previews results at 1K in about five seconds on a phone or laptop.
