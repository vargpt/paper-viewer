Where Does an AI "See" Race in a Chest X-ray?

AI models can guess a patient's self-reported race from a chest X-ray — something radiologists can't do. This paper tests eight popular "saliency" tools to find where in the image that information lives, and asks a harder question: can we even trust these tools?

Based on: Konate, Lebrat, Santa Cruz, Gichoya, Price, Seyyed-Kalantari, Fookes, Bradley & Salvado. "Interpretability of AI race detection model in medical imaging with saliency methods." Computational and Structural Biotechnology Journal, 2025.

1. The problem: a hidden signal with real consequences

Deep neural networks (DNNs) are increasingly used to read medical images. But to doctors they are often a "black box": they give an answer without a simple explanation of why.

An earlier study by Gichoya et al. showed something unsettling: AI models can predict a patient's race from medical images with very high accuracy — across modalities, tasks and datasets — even though no human radiologist can do this, and even though the models were not built to look for race. That study tried one explanation tool (Grad-CAM) and found no consistent region explaining the prediction.

Why it matters: DNNs can be unfair in disease diagnosis — for example, disproportionately reporting Black patients as healthy compared with White patients, which can lead to denial of needed care. If a model quietly uses race information when diagnosing disease, clinicians may not be able to judge whether that's appropriate.

The paper defines unfairness as differences in performance for or against a subgroup — e.g. lower disease-diagnosis accuracy for one racial, age or sex group than another.

This paper's goal: use explainability tools to find the subtle cues a DNN uses to detect race in chest X-rays — and, crucially, to evaluate whether those tools actually work.

3. The key idea: a "keep it / remove it" test

There's no ground truth telling us where race "is" in an X-ray. So how do you grade a saliency method? The authors propose a practical definition:

An optimal saliency method finds a region that (1) lets the network still predict correctly when it sees only that region, and (2) misleads the network when that region is removed.

They measure both with AUC (area under the ROC curve) on a Black-vs-White classification. AUC = 1 means perfect separation; AUC = 0.5 means coin-flipping. The ideal method: mask alone → AUC ≈ 1, mask removed → AUC ≈ 0.5.

In the paper, "keep mask only" sets everything outside the mask to 0 (black), and "remove mask" fills the mask region with mid-gray (0.5). The authors tried two mask sizes — 15% and 30% of pixels — motivated by prior work (Fong et al.) showing that ~15% of the most informative pixels can be as effective as the whole image, and that perturbing ~20% can have a similar effect to perturbing all of it.

Images with visible medical devices or clinically significant findings were removed after visual inspection. Validation AUCs (on 10,000 scans) were 0.92, 0.93 and 0.95 for Asian, Black and White. Because the Asian group was small, the saliency analysis focuses on Black vs. White.

  1. Build an atlas. Align all X-rays to a common "average chest" (details below), so maps from different people can be compared pixel by pixel.
  2. Compute saliency maps for every image with each method, and threshold them into binary masks (top 15% or 30% of pixels).
  3. Average the masks per group. Each subject's mask is warped into atlas space and averaged into a group saliency map.
  4. Warp the group mask back onto each patient's original image, giving a patient-specific mask.
  5. Re-classify twice: keeping only the mask, and removing the mask. Compare AUCs to the original.

5. The atlas: an "average chest" for each group

Using the ANTs software package, the authors registered (aligned) images in three successive stages:

They started from a random image per group, registered all others to it, averaged them into a new atlas, and repeated this five times for each stage. Images where registration clearly failed were removed by visual check. The result: an atlas for the whole population and one per race group.

Are the group averages actually different?

Subtracting atlases shows where groups differ in brightness. To check whether differences are real or just noise, the authors ran a permutation test:

z = (actual difference − mean of shuffled differences) ÷ std of shuffled differences

In plain words: They formed 1,000 random "fake" pairs of groups, each mixing 50 White and 50 Black images, so any difference between them should be about zero. This gives, for every pixel, how big a difference you'd expect by chance (mean and spread). Then the real difference between 100 White and 100 Black images is expressed in units of that chance spread — a z-score. A z of 5 means "five times bigger than random variation would typically produce."

What they found: differences were highly significant, with large areas beyond ±5 standard deviations. Comparing White and Black atlases, the trachea, shoulders and neck appear brighter for White (and Asian) scans — suggesting more X-ray absorption or denser tissue there — while the area below the diaphragm is brighter for Black scans, especially compared with White scans. The authors note that the causes of these intensity differences were not explored.

6. Results: which saliency method passes the test?

Looking at average maps, most methods agreed the trachea, parts of the shoulders and parts of the lungs were most salient. Extremal Perturbation (which takes a target area) was run at 5%, 10%, 20%, 30% and 40%: above 20% it highlighted the lungs; at smaller sizes it highlighted the trachea for Asian and Black scans and the lung for White scans.

But "looks plausible" isn't the same as "is correct." Switch thresholds to see how each method fared on the keep/remove test. Original whole-image AUC (Black vs. White): 0.98.

The three "winners" at 30%: AUC when their mask is removed

Values as reported in the paper's text. Lower is better here — closer to 0.5 means removing the region left the network guessing.

Headline: At 30%, every method except Perturbation produced masks that predicted race well on their own (AUC above 0.75). But only Rise also drove the network to pure chance (AUC 0.5) when its region was removed. GradCAM (0.53) and Linear (0.57) came close.

What did the good maps highlight?

  • GradCAM & Linear looked similar: trachea and lungs. Black scans showed more pronounced neck areas and fully visible lungs; White scans highlighted the left shoulder and inner right lung.
  • Rise gave a fragmented map. For White scans: both shoulders, right lung, part of the trachea, upper lung. For Black scans: right lung, part of the trachea, upper lung, and a small patch at the bottom of the chest. It also emphasised scan discontinuities relevant to the prediction.
  • Perturbation 40 did the reverse of what you'd want: its mask alone gave AUC 0.5, while removing it left accuracy as good as the original — and its region largely didn't overlap with Rise and GradCAM.

These regions line up with the atlas differences: the biggest group differences were in the upper part of the scans and the trachea — the same areas the network relied on. The authors suggest the intensity difference might be a simple proxy the network uses.

7. A clue: the network may use "low-frequency" features

Why did a good explanation need a large (30%) and scattered region? The authors hypothesise that the network relies on low-frequency features — broad, smooth variations like overall brightness of regions — so race information is spread across the whole image rather than sitting in one spot. Humans are more sensitive to contrast than to absolute intensity, so such cues would be nearly invisible to us.

To probe this, they computed the DC gain of the network's convolution filters: how strongly a filter responds to a perfectly flat, constant input. For a filter, that's simply the sum of its weights. A filter with zero DC gain ignores overall brightness; a non-zero one responds to it.

8. Limitations and cautions

No ground truth. Nobody knows where race information "really" is in an X-ray, so the keep/remove AUC test is a proxy for faithfulness, not a definitive answer.
Methods disagree. Even the successful methods (Rise, GradCAM, Linear) highlight different regions, making a reliable anatomical explanation hard.
Thresholding can bias results. Gradient methods assign nearly equal importance to many scattered pixels; picking the "top" ones can distort their AUC scores.
Mask size matters. Conclusions changed between 15% and 30%, and saliency methods assume a single focused region — which the network may not use.
Self-identified labels. Race labels are self-reported and may not match biological ancestry (e.g. someone identifying as White may be bi-racial). No clear anatomical chest differences between races are established in the literature or by clinicians.
Scope. One dataset (MIMIC-CXR), one model (ResNet34); the small Asian cohort was left out of saliency analysis; causes of intensity differences weren't explored; the low-frequency analysis is preliminary.

The authors cite the classic "husky vs. wolf" example: a model that seemed to recognise wolves was actually using snow in the background. Saliency maps assume networks reason like humans, focusing on a specific object — but networks may exploit highly predictive cues humans can't perceive.

9. Takeaways

  • 🔍 Rise worked best: at a 30% mask size, its region alone predicted race well and its removal left the network at chance (AUC 0.5).
  • 📏 Size of the salient region is crucial. A meaningful explanation for race required a large chunk of the image.
  • 🆕 Newer isn't better. The most recent methods did not necessarily give the most faithful explanations — likely because they were designed for everyday photos with clear objects, not medical images.
  • 🌫️ Low-level intensity cues may drive race prediction; the authors recommend data augmentation to stop networks from learning irrelevant gray-level patterns.
  • ⚠️ Use saliency maps in medicine with caution, and evaluate them rigorously before deployment. Task- and model-specific interpretability methods are the suggested way forward.

The authors see such evaluated interpretability tools as useful during model validation to detect demographic-specific biases, and potentially to give clinicians transparency about which image regions drive AI predictions.
