What Do Radiologists Actually Want From “Explainable” AI?

1. The problem: good AI that nobody uses

Machine-learning (ML) models can classify images, outline tumours (segmentation), and even draft radiology reports, sometimes performing on par with radiologists. Yet they have not been widely adopted in real clinical work.

The paper points to one major reason: lack of explainability. If a radiologist can’t understand why a model said “tumour”, they can’t check it, and they stay out of the loop. The authors describe explainability as one ingredient of the broader idea of trust, alongside performance, confidence and fairness.

The gap the paper targets: Many explanation tools have been built from the developers’ point of view, with little systematic checking by clinicians. Earlier user studies existed, but each focused on a single imaging modality or use case (e.g. chest X-rays, brain tumour grading, prostate lesions). The authors describe their work as, to their knowledge, the first radiologist-centred study spanning multiple imaging modalities and clinical tasks.

Their plan: ask radiologists directly, analyse the answers (including separating junior residents from senior radiologists), and turn the findings into practical design guidelines for ML researchers.

The questionnaire was built in Microsoft Forms and distributed within a university Department of Radiology, reaching radiologists from different hospitals in one metropolitan area. In 11 of the multiple-choice questions an “other” box let people add their own answer. Click a section to see what it asked.

Who answered?

Experience mix. Residents were the largest group (43%), and senior radiologists with more than 10 years of experience the second largest (30%). So the sample is weighted toward the two ends of the experience spectrum, useful for comparing novices with veterans.

Most participants worked in Abdominal and Neuroimaging divisions, and CT and MRI were the most common modalities.

ML familiarity. 52% had no significant ML experience, and 26% knew ML concepts at a basic level. Only a few had applied ML in research or practice. Most had basic statistics knowledge.

Takeaway: most respondents were not familiar with how ML models work internally. That is exactly the audience explanations must serve.

4. Results: what radiologists want

4.1 Where should ML help?

43 of 46 said they are interested in using ML in their clinical workflow, mostly to increase efficiency and speed; some also said it could eventually improve accuracy.

The most frequently chosen tasks were:

  • Workflow management
  • Performing routine and tedious diagnostic tasks
  • Flagging urgent / emergent findings

Very few chose “aiding in diagnosing rare or difficult cases.”

In open-ended answers, the most common response (13%) was improving efficiency, for example reporting normal cases without significant findings. Others mentioned acting as a double-reader, catching subtle findings, segmentation and flagging urgent cases. Participants said they hoped, but weren’t sure, ML could help with differential diagnosis, rare cases and report preparation.

In short, radiologists mostly want ML as a “helping hand” for routine work, freeing up their time for the hard cases, rather than as an oracle for the hardest ones.

4.2 How do radiologists explain things to each other?

Before asking what AI should do, the survey asked what radiologists themselves use to justify a diagnosis to colleagues or physicians.

Separately, 18 participants said examples of similar cases/patients “sometimes” help their decisions, and 15 said they use them “in most cases.” This hints at why showing representative cases (prototypes) might be useful.

Why do they want explainability?

The most common reason was “to have confidence and trust in using the ML model.” Other frequent reasons were to understand and validate the features driving the model’s output and to express a degree of certainty in the radiological report.

4.3 What makes a model explainable? Residents vs. seniors

This is a key finding. Toggle between groups to compare the percentages reported in the paper.

Only factors for which the paper reports exact percentages are plotted. The paper also says both groups chose “generating textual explanations” and “showing representative cases,” and that “counterfactual explanations” was the least-selected option overall (33%).

The surprise: SRTCS. Standardized Radiologic Terminology and Classification Systems are the scoring systems radiologists already use, such as BI-RADS for breast imaging. 86% of senior radiologists picked “offering SRTCS” as important for explainability, while no residents did. Seniors want the model to speak their established language.

Overall, senior radiologists considered a broader range of factors necessary than residents did. Both groups ranked “focusing on relevant features” (e.g. radiomics, which are quantitative imaging features) and “visual heatmaps of model attention” highly.

5. Explainability and trust

42 / 46say explainability is a prerequisite for trust

Aspects most often seen as boosting both explainability and trust were transparent data preprocessing and curation, transparent model training (e.g. hyperparameter fine-tuning), and a feedback mechanism that lets users adjust predictions. Being integrated into existing software (e.g. an image viewer) was not widely chosen as a factor for explainability or trust.

Try it: the model disagrees with you

You’re a radiologist. An ML model’s diagnosis contradicts yours. What do you do? Pick an answer, then see what participants said.

Most participants said they would not completely reject the model. Instead they would seek more information before deciding:

  • 16 residents and 10 senior radiologists would analyse the model’s explanations.
  • Unlike residents, a high number of senior radiologists would also consult colleagues or the relevant literature.
  • Only 3 participants said they would not trust the model diagnosis at all.

This is why explanations matter: they are the evidence radiologists reach for when they disagree with the model.

6. Evaluation and deployment concerns

Major concerns raised were bias in data/training (a fairness risk), lack of trust, lack of explainability, and insufficient accuracy. Senior radiologists were far more worried about bias:

Only half of the seniors believe an explainable model can better reveal data or model bias; the other half are unsure. The authors flag this as needing further investigation.

How they judge an ML model

In order of priority: (1) task performance, (2) the model’s confidence in its results, (3) the quality of its explanations in highlighting task-relevant features.

Two notable findings:
  • About 70% said a model performing similar to radiologists (not necessarily better) could be integrated, provided other requirements are met.
  • Unexpectedly, most said a model that consistently performs comparably to radiologists but lacks explainability could still potentially be incorporated. And while software integration wasn’t seen as boosting trust, 65% called it a necessary requirement for clinical use (e.g. inside PACS, the Picture Archiving and Communication System).

8. How to grade an explanation

The authors propose four metrics for assessing an explainability tool: alignment with clinical evidence, fidelity, robustness, and effect on radiologist performance. The two toys below illustrate the first two ideas.

Note: these are simplified, made-up illustrations of the concepts. They are not data or models from the paper.

Alignment: does the heatmap overlap the radiologist’s outline?

The green outline is a radiologist’s manual segmentation mask. The paper suggests reporting the overlap between explanation-highlighted areas and such radiologist-provided information.

Fidelity: does hiding the highlighted area change the prediction?

In this toy, the “model” secretly relies on the lower-right region. Perturbing a highlighted feature and watching the prediction is the fidelity test the paper describes: a large impact means the explanation really reflects what the model used.

Why fidelity matters for bias: if an explanation faithfully shows everything the model uses, including spurious features, it could expose hidden data biases, partly addressing the seniors’ top concern. Whether such spurious correlations really correspond to data biases needs significant experiments, the authors note.

Robustness: explanations should stay consistent across settings, such as scanner shifts. One practical check is to add adversarial noise to training data and see whether the explanation changes much; insignificant changes suggest some robustness.

Effect on radiologist performance: ultimately, measure whether the explanation improves the user’s performance in the clinical task, for example by identifying accurate imaging features to include in reports.

9. Limitations (as stated by the authors)

  • Small sample. The number of participants was low; more responses would improve generalizability.
  • Trust under-explored. Lack of trust emerged as a major concern among senior radiologists, but the survey did not include enough questions on trust.
  • Confidence not treated as explanation. Participants rated model confidence as important for evaluation, but the survey wasn’t designed to treat it as a form of explainability; this is left to future work.
  • Participants came from hospitals in a single metropolitan area, through one university radiology department.
  • Survey data and analysis code are stated to be released upon acceptance.
Bottom line. The participating radiologists defined explainability as a model’s ability to focus on clinically relevant imaging features and provide multiple forms of explanation: SRTCS scores, visual heatmaps and textual reasoning. Senior radiologists were more conservative and more concerned about bias. The authors argue that earning their trust should be a top priority in model development.
