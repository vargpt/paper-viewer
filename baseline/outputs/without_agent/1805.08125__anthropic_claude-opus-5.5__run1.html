A Marketplace for Data:
An Algorithmic Solution

How do you build a real-time market that sells training data for machine learning — when data can be copied for free, its value depends on what other data you have, and nobody knows in advance which dataset will actually help?

1. Why selling data is hard

Businesses increasingly need machine-learning predictions, often in real time (a ride-share company setting prices, a retailer sending targeted coupons). Good predictions need good training data, which firms often can't collect themselves — and real-time data gets "stale" quickly. So the authors ask: what would a market for training data look like?

The paper identifies four features that make data unlike other goods. Click each to expand.

i
Freely replicable
Data can be copied at zero marginal cost. You can't simply "give it to the highest bidder" the way you would a single item.
ii
Combinatorial value
A dataset's worth depends on which other (possibly correlated) datasets are also available. Two near-identical datasets are not worth twice as much as one.
iii
Tasks vary wildly
A 10% accuracy gain means very different things to a hedge fund maximizing profit versus a logistics company cutting inventory costs.
iv
Hard to verify upfront
You can't tell if a dataset (say, satellite images) helps your specific task until you actually train a model on it.

Why not just reuse existing online markets?

Online ad auctions

Ad space is not replicable — one ad per slot — so a second-price auction to the highest bidder works. Advertisers also have strong priors (e.g. historical click-through rates). Data buyers have neither: data is copyable, and they have no prior on how useful a particular dataset is for their task.

Prediction markets

"Experts" sell probability forecasts for a specific variable and get paid when the outcome is revealed. But they must choose which tasks to predict. A data seller (e.g. a store selling foot-traffic) cannot know which buyer's task its data will help.

What's needed is a market that matches prediction tasks to datasets based on which data, collectively, is empirically most predictive — and cheap enough for the buyer.

Running example: inventory optimization

Sellers are retail stores selling anonymized minute-by-minute foot-traffic streams. Buyers are logistics companies wanting to forecast inventory demand. A logistics company can't sensibly bid on each store's stream — but it does know its cost model for bad forecasts, so it can say: "I'm willing to pay $1000 for each percentage-point increase in forecasting accuracy." That single number is the key to the whole design.

2. The model: who's in the market

M Sellers

Each seller j supplies one feature Xj — a data vector of length T (e.g. one store's foot-traffic time series). Sellers can't improve their data's quality, don't know the buyers' tasks, have no privacy concerns (data is assumed anonymized), and simply want revenue.

N Buyers (arriving one at a time)

Buyer n has a prediction task Yn (e.g. historical demand), a private value μn for a unit increase in accuracy, and submits a public bid bn (which may differ from μn if the buyer is strategic).

Accuracy is measured by a gain function G ∈ [0,1] (for example 1 − normalized RMSE for regression, or accuracy for classification). The buyer's value from a prediction is simply:

value = μn · G(Yn, Ŷn)

In words: "what I'm willing to pay per unit of accuracy" × "how accurate the prediction turned out". The authors flag this as a key modeling decision: buyers value accuracy, not particular datasets. Non-linearities (e.g. 50% accuracy on a balanced coin-flip task being worthless) can be folded into G.

The marketplace holds a single scalar price pn, an ML algorithm M (could just be an off-the-shelf library), and four functions it must design:

FunctionWhat it does
AF AllocationGiven price and bid, decides the quality of the data the buyer gets (e.g. by adding noise).
RF RevenueDecides how much to charge the buyer.

A nice side-effect (Remark 2.4): buyers never see the raw features — they only receive predictions from a model trained on them. This sidesteps sellers' fear that once data is released, it can be copied endlessly.

3. What a good data market must achieve

✓
Truthful
Bidding your real value μn should be the best strategy: μn = argmaxz U(z, Yn).
$
Revenue maximizing
Over a worst-case sequence of buyers (no distributional assumptions), average revenue should approach that of the best fixed price in hindsight — i.e. average regret → 0.
⚖
Fair split
Revenue divided by Shapley fairness (balance, symmetry, zero element, additivity) — and robust to sellers copying their data.
⚡
Efficient
Each step runs in polynomial time in M (number of sellers) and doesn't grow with N (buyers seen so far).

The tension: combinatorial goods usually make these goals blow up exponentially. Exact Shapley values cost Θ(2M); a naive Multiplicative Weights over per-dataset price vectors costs Θ(exp(M)); and prior work shows regret minimization for even simple non-additive valuations is computationally intractable.

The key simplification: keep one scalar price for all the data, and treat the data on sale as a single pool of "information" whose quality is turned up or down. Because buyers are described by a scalar μn, a scalar price is natural.

4. Allocation and pricing that make honesty optimal

Allocation: degrade data when the bid is below the price

If the bid bn ≥ price pn, the buyer gets the data untouched. If bn < pn, the data is degraded in proportion to the gap. Two examples from the paper:

Real-valued data:   X̃j(t) = Xj(t) + max(0, pn − bn) · N(0, σ²)
Binary data:   X̃j(t) = B(t; θ) · Xj(t),   θ = min(bn/pn, 1)  (random masking)

Either way: bid less than the price, and you get noisier data, hence a worse prediction.

Revenue: Myerson's payment rule

Let h(z) be the accuracy the buyer would get if they bid z (with price fixed). The charge is:

RF*(pn, bn, Yn) = bn · h(bn)  −  ∫0bn h(z) dz

In words: start with "your bid × the accuracy you got" (the rectangle), then refund the area under the accuracy curve up to your bid. What you pay is the sliver above the curve. Theorem 5.1: as long as more bid never means worse accuracy (Assumption 1, monotonicity), this makes bidding your true μn optimal — and truthfulness is achievable only if that monotonicity holds.

Why not just a take-it-or-leave-it price? Myerson's classic result says that for single-parameter buyers, one posted price is optimal. The paper points out this fails here because buyers are also characterized by their task Yn: if dataset X1 only helps task Y1 and X2 only helps Y2, the optimal scheme prices them differently at μ1 and μ2. Finding the revenue-optimal allocation function is left open.

5. Learning the right price over time

The price must be fixed before each buyer arrives (otherwise truthfulness breaks). Too high a price → data gets heavily degraded → the "b·h(b)" term shrinks. Too low → the refund integral is large. Either way revenue is lost.

Algorithm 1 uses Multiplicative Weights: lay a grid of candidate prices (an ε-net of the bid range) — each one an "expert". Pick a price at random in proportion to its weight. After the buyer's bid arrives, compute what each candidate price would have earned, and boost its weight:

wi ← wi · (1 + δ · RF*(ci, bn, Yn) / Bmax)

Prices that would have earned more grow in weight and get chosen more often. Theorem 5.2 bounds the average regret against the best fixed price in hindsight by O(√(log N / N)) — it goes to zero, and crucially the bound does not depend on the number of sellers M.

6. Splitting the revenue fairly among sellers

The buyer pays one lump sum for an accuracy gain. How much of it does each store's data deserve?

The naive idea fails: leave-one-out

"Value of feature m = accuracy with everything − accuracy without m." With two identical features, removing either loses nothing, so both get zero — even if together they're very predictive.

Shapley values

The Shapley value (the unique split satisfying balance, symmetry, zero-element and additivity) averages a feature's marginal contribution over every possible order in which features could be added:

ψ(m) = ΣT ⊆ [M]∖{m}   |T|!(M−|T|−1)! / M!  ·  [ G(with T ∪ {m}) − G(with T) ]

In words: imagine sellers joining in a random order; pay each seller the average accuracy bump they bring when they join. Exact computation takes Θ(2M) — hopeless for many sellers.

Algorithm 2 (Shapley-Approx) instead samples K random orderings and averages. Theorem 5.3: with K > M·log(2/δ)/(2ε²), the estimate is within ε of the true Shapley value for every feature, with probability 1−δ.

7. The copying attack — and a new kind of fairness

Here's a problem specific to freely replicable goods. Two sellers, A and B, sell identical data. Shapley gives each ½. Now A uploads a copy of her own data under a new name. Accuracy doesn't change (the copy adds no information), so the buyer pays the same — but Shapley now sees three identical features and gives A two of them: A gets ⅔, B drops to ⅓. Copying is free, so this is a disaster.

The paper's new property, robustness to replication (Property 3.4): a seller's total payout across the original and all copies must not exceed (up to ε) what they'd get without copying.

Algorithm 3 (Shapley-Robust) exploits a special feature of data: you can measure how similar two datasets are (e.g. cosine similarity). It shrinks each feature's approximate Shapley value by its total similarity to all other features:

ψ(m) = ψ̂(m) · exp( −λ · Σj ≠ m SM(Xm, Xj) )

In words: the more your data resembles other data on sale, the more your share is discounted. Copies are maximally similar to each other, so copying is penalized; unique data is barely touched.

Try the attack. Seller A and seller B hold identical data. Choose how many extra copies A uploads.

With λ = 1 and one copy, A's payout falls from 1/(2e) to 2/(3e²), matching the paper's Example 4.5.

How strong must the penalty be?

In the proof of Theorem 5.4, robustness boils down to requiring c · e−λ(c−1) ≤ 1 for every number of copies c — which holds when λ = log 2. More generally, Proposition 5.2 says a penalty function f (of a feature's total similarity x) is robust if and only if

(c + 1) · f(x + c) ≤ f(x)    for all copies c ∈ {1,2,…}

In words: making c extra copies multiplies your number of payout slots by c+1, so the penalty must shrink each slot by at least that factor. An appendix argument suggests exponential penalties are, loosely, the "least harsh" ones that work.

An impossibility

Proposition 5.1: if seller identities are anonymous, you cannot have both balance (payouts sum exactly to the revenue) and robustness to replication. Why? The market cannot tell "A plus A's copy plus B" apart from "three honest sellers A, B, C with identical data". Balance forces ⅓ each in the second case, so it must also give A ⅔ in the first. Algorithm 3 therefore keeps symmetry, zero-element and additivity (to ε precision) but gives up exact balance — some revenue goes unallocated when data is redundant.

A side benefit: because penalties are "local", sellers are nudged to provide data that is predictive for many tasks and uncorrelated with what's already on sale.

The authors also suggest the permutation-sampling approach to Shapley values, with its finite-sample guarantee, could serve as a model-agnostic tool for feature importance in ML generally — and that the replication-robust fairness idea applies to other digital goods, such as battery-cost attribution among smartphone apps or rewarding experts in prediction markets.

9. Limitations and open questions

  • Single scalar price, fixed degradation. All features are degraded by the same scaling. An adaptive allocation function tuned to prediction tasks might earn more; the optimal allocation function is left open (Remark 4.2).
  • Myopic buyers. Each buyer visits once; multi-stage strategic behavior isn't studied (Remark 2.6).
  • No exclusivity externalities. A hedge fund might pay a premium for a dataset that only it gets; the model ignores how a buyer's utility depends on who else receives the data (Remark 2.7).
  • Simplified sellers. Sellers can't improve data quality, have no privacy concerns, and incur no acquisition cost. Incorporating privacy, feedback and costs is future work.
  • Buyers can't alter their task. Truthfulness assumes buyers report Yn honestly; only the bid is strategic.
  • Balance is sacrificed. The robust split doesn't distribute all revenue; which robust mechanism is "most balance-preserving" is open.
  • Centralized pricing. The market, not individual sellers, sets prices (Remark 2.5) — a modeling choice made for tractability.
  • Theory only. Guarantees are proved under the stated assumptions; the paper does not report empirical evaluations.
